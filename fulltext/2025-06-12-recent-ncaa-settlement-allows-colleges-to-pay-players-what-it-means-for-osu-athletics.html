Recent NCAA settlement allows colleges to pay players: What it means for OSU athletics

By Timothy Christensen  /  The O'Colly  /  2025-06-12
This is an archived copy preserved for Timothy Christensen's portfolio. The original was published in The O'Colly at this link; this copy is shown because the original page is no longer reachable.

Shockwaves were sent across the entire collegiate sports world June 6 when Judge Claudia Wilken approved a settlement in the House V. NCAA case, allowing colleges to put money directly into athletes' pockets.

The days of amateurism in college sports have dissipated as colleges will now be paying not only future athletes but also those who played in the past.

The settlement includes $2.8 billion in back damages to be paid over the next 10 years to any athlete who competed in college from 2016-2024. Plaintiffs argued that the NCAA illegally limited the athletes' opportunities to make money off their name image and likeness.

While that is a lot of money, the NCAA President Charlie Baker claimed this was the best option for the association:

“It is an opportunity for the D-I community to pay for back damages over 10 years instead of triple that amount all at once,” Baker said during a keynote address Tuesday in Orlando, Florida. “It creates a future that comes with choices instead of bankruptcy."

The NCAA’s legal troubles are not completely over yet, though. It still has a lawsuit pending on whether student athletes need to be treated as employees and provided the benefits that come with that, including minimum wage.

The NCAA’s survival is not the only impact that this settlement will have on college sports; it's changing programs altogether.

Colleges will now be able to pay their athletes directly, limiting the need to go through back channels like NIL to entice them into joining programs.

To keep payments from getting outlandish, colleges will be limited to a spending starting at about $20.5 million. It's expected to rise every year based on the revenue college sports collect.

Despite NIL no longer being the only form of payment in college sports, it is not going anywhere. NIL money will not be included toward a team’s $20.5 million cap, so it will most likely still be utilized.

However, the plan is to more closely regulate NIL money. All NIL deals of more than $600 will need to go through a clearinghouse to validate that players get the correct value for their endorsement or promotion.

The goal of a spending cap is to keep parity in college sports. The cap will prevent highly-funded programs from being able to buy championship rosters.

While big brands will always have an advantage, a cap should create advantages for smart programs — those that know which players to pay and at what price. This is the system that Oklahoma State and figures like athletic director Chad Weiberg will have to navigate.

The first problem that OSU will need to deal with is finding the $20.5 million to make the most of the cap.

OSU has already made attempts at funding its NIL initiatives with ventures like the Boys From Oklahoma concert in April and adding QR codes to football players' helmets. More outside-the-box thinking will be expected from OSU in the future.

OSU will now need to evaluate money dispersion between sports programs. With high-revenue teams on campus colliding with historically successful non-revenue programs such as wrestling and golf, deciding how much money each sport gets will be a critical task for the university and its boosters.

One thing is clear: College sports will never be the same.

There will be many tough decisions made by executives, coaches, players and donors moving forward, meaning the time of mourning the old college sports system is over. Those who accept the challenge of the future the fastest will have the best chance at success, despite the uncertainty.
